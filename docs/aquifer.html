Why not yet: the signal is smaller than the noise

Radar interferometry (InSAR) compares the phase of the radar echo from two passes of the satellite over the same ground. If the ground moved toward or away from the satellite between the passes, the phase shifts. The atmosphere, the ionosphere and changes at the surface shift it too, so every pair of passes carries some noise. To see the ground sinking, the sinking has to add up to more than that noise.

Irrigation shows up as false movement

Over a field, the radar echo comes partly from the soil and the crop. When a field is irrigated, the soil gets wet and the crop grows, which changes how the radar wave travels into them. That shifts the phase even though the ground has not moved. Because the change points the same way pass after pass, adding pairs up makes it look like steady sinking.

No displacement map of fields is published. Values summed from short pairs over farms would be false movement from water in the soil, not ground motion.

How long until sinking could be seen

It depends on how the radar pairs are processed. Today's NISAR interferogram products pair each pass with the next one. Chaining those short pairs adds up their noise and any bias. A network that also pairs passes months apart follows slow movement directly, with much less build-up. A full year is the shortest record that can separate sinking from the yearly cycle of soil moisture.
